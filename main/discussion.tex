\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main lesson of APT-Bench is not that a single architecture solves multi-cancer liquid biopsy. It is that this problem decomposes into targets with different statistical units, different failure modes, and different evidentiary standards. Once those distinctions are enforced, the story becomes clearer. Coarse lineage prediction is measurably easier than fine subtype prediction. Disease recognition is extremely strong within cohort but vulnerable to patient-specific absolute-expression effects. The same disease signal can be recovered with compact aptamer panels and few sampled cells per patient, indicating measurement efficiency but not unique causal biomarker discovery.

This framing also clarifies what \ourmethod{} does and does not establish. The model provides a statistically supported improvement over strong classical baselines on coarse-lineage macro-F1. It does not significantly beat XGBoost on fine-subtype macro-F1, and its tail advantage is not stable enough to serve as a headline claim. We view that as a strength of the benchmark rather than a weakness of the paper: the benchmark prevents us from turning a modest, structure-specific gain into an unjustified universal claim.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\paragraph{Disease-level novelty is exploratory.}
We report leave-one-disease-out novelty detection only in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:novelty}. This track is useful as a stress test, but the small number of disease groups and limited proxy-novel diversity make it too fragile to serve as a main contribution.

\paragraph{The disease result is within-cohort and potentially confounded.}
The cohort contains only 40 patients, and acquisition-batch metadata are unavailable. The strong patient-fingerprint diagnostic and the degradation under patient-wise centering show that disease recognition may depend partly on technical or patient-specific offsets that are inseparable in the current dataset.

\paragraph{Method ranking depends on metric choice.}
DropCascade's strongest evidence is at the coarse level, whereas XGBoost remains highly competitive on fine subtype prediction. We therefore caution against compressing the benchmark into a single scalar ranking.

\paragraph{Compact panels imply measurement efficiency, not unique biomarkers.}
The small-panel and equal-cell subsampling analyses show that strong within-cohort disease recognition can be preserved with far fewer aptamers and few sampled cells per patient. This should be read as evidence that predictive signal is distributed and that measurement budgets can be reduced, not as proof that a universal causal biomarker set has been identified.

Overall, the benchmark suggests a practical research agenda. Future work should test external cohorts with batch metadata, strengthen subtype-level hierarchy modeling, and evaluate whether compact APT panels remain measurement-efficient outside this cohort.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced APT-Bench, a patient-disjoint benchmark for multi-resolution learning from single-cell aptamer liquid-biopsy data, and used it to separate three questions that are often conflated: cell-level coarse-to-fine hierarchy prediction, patient-level disease recognition under the correct statistical unit, and measurement-efficient disease recognition under restricted aptamer and cell-count budgets. This separation matters because the modality supports these tasks unevenly. Within cohort, disease recognition is extremely strong and can be preserved with compact aptamer panels and few sampled cells per patient, but sensitivity analyses show that this signal depends heavily on patient-specific absolute-expression structure and therefore should not be overclaimed as solved clinical generalization. In contrast, patient-disjoint cell-level hierarchy prediction remains difficult, with a persistent gap between coarse lineage and fine subtype recognition.

Within this benchmark, \ourmethod{} serves as a hierarchy-aware reference model rather than a universal winner. Its strongest evidence is a statistically supported gain in coarse-lineage macro-F1 over strong classical baselines. Its fine-subtype performance remains competitive with, but not significantly superior to, XGBoost. We view this as the right scientific outcome: the benchmark forces a more disciplined account of what is genuinely improved, what remains unresolved, and which claims the current evidence does not support.
