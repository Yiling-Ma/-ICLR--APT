\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\paragraph{Novelty detection targets disease-level, not subtype-level, shift.}
Our novelty-detection protocol (\S\ref{sec:method:novelty}) is built around a specific, clinically motivated notion of novelty: a cell drawn from a patient with a disease category entirely absent from training. This is a coarse, \emph{patient-level} shift, and it is not the same problem as detecting a genuinely \emph{unseen cell subtype} within an otherwise known lineage. It is useful to distinguish two conceptually different kinds of shift that a deployed system may encounter: a \emph{semantic in-taxonomy shift}, in which a cell's fine-grained subtype was never observed during training even though its coarse lineage is known, and a \emph{covariate or domain shift}, in which the cell's subtype is entirely familiar but it originates from a new patient, disease, or acquisition context. A clinically useful system should reject the former, ideally retaining the correct coarse lineage rather than discarding the input outright, while accepting the latter rather than flagging every cell from an unfamiliar patient as novel. Our leave-one-disease-out protocol evaluates only the covariate/domain axis: every cell held out as ``novel'' has a disease label absent from training, but its lineage and, in the large majority of cases, its fine-grained subtype are already present in the training set under other diseases. Consequently, our reported novelty-detection results (\S\ref{sec:results:novelty}) do not establish that \ourmethod{} can detect a truly unseen subtype, correctly retain its lineage rather than rejecting it outright, or avoid mistaking a known subtype from an unfamiliar patient for a novel one. Evaluating this semantic in-taxonomy axis directly would require a leave-one-subtype-out protocol with an explicit partial-rejection mechanism, along the lines of recent hierarchical selective classification and hierarchical out-of-distribution methods \citep{goren2024hierarchical, wallin2025prohoc}, together with a matched control that verifies known subtypes from held-out patients or diseases are not falsely rejected. We view this as an important extension of the present disease-level formulation rather than a claim already supported by the current experiments, and leave it to future work.
