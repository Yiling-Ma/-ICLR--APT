\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main lesson of APT-Bench is not that a single architecture solves multi-cancer liquid biopsy. It is that this problem decomposes into targets with different statistical units, different failure modes, and different evidentiary standards. Once those distinctions are enforced, the story becomes clearer. Disease recognition is extremely strong within cohort but vulnerable to patient-specific absolute-expression effects. Coarse lineage prediction is measurably easier than fine subtype prediction. Novelty detection is necessary because unseen-disease transfer remains poor even when in-distribution cell-level performance appears competitive.

This framing also clarifies what \ourmethod{} does and does not establish. The model provides a statistically supported improvement over strong classical baselines on coarse-lineage macro-F1 and a clear improvement in trained novelty detection over training-free confidence scores. It does not significantly beat XGBoost on fine-subtype macro-F1, and its tail advantage is not stable enough to serve as a headline claim. We view that as a strength of the benchmark rather than a weakness of the paper: the benchmark prevents us from turning a modest, structure-specific gain into an unjustified universal claim.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\paragraph{Disease-level novelty is not subtype-level novelty.}
Our novelty formulation treats an unseen disease as novel. This is clinically meaningful, but it is not the same as encountering a truly unseen cell subtype within a known lineage. Evaluating that setting would require leave-one-subtype-out protocols with partial rejection at internal nodes of the hierarchy.

\paragraph{The disease result is within-cohort and potentially confounded.}
The cohort contains only 40 patients, and acquisition-batch metadata are unavailable. The strong patient-fingerprint diagnostic and the degradation under patient-wise centering show that disease recognition may depend partly on technical or patient-specific offsets that are inseparable in the current dataset.

\paragraph{Method ranking depends on metric choice.}
DropCascade's strongest evidence is at the coarse level and in novelty detection, whereas XGBoost remains highly competitive on fine subtype prediction. We therefore caution against compressing the benchmark into a single scalar ranking.

\paragraph{Compact panels imply measurement efficiency, not unique biomarkers.}
The small-panel disease analysis shows that strong within-cohort disease recognition can be preserved with far fewer aptamers. This should be read as evidence that predictive signal is distributed and that measurement budgets can be reduced, not as proof that a universal causal biomarker set has been identified.

Overall, the benchmark suggests a practical research agenda. Future work should test external cohorts with batch metadata, introduce subtype-level novelty protocols, and explore hierarchy-aware models that improve fine subtype recognition without relying on leakage-prone evaluation regimes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced APT-Bench, a patient-disjoint benchmark for multi-resolution learning from single-cell aptamer liquid-biopsy data, and used it to separate three questions that are often conflated: patient-level disease recognition, cell-level hierarchical annotation, and novelty awareness under unseen disease shift. This separation matters because the modality supports these tasks unevenly. Within cohort, disease recognition is extremely strong and can be preserved with compact aptamer panels, but sensitivity analyses show that this signal depends heavily on patient-specific absolute-expression structure and therefore should not be overclaimed as solved clinical generalization. In contrast, patient-disjoint cell-level hierarchy prediction remains difficult, with a persistent gap between coarse lineage and fine subtype recognition.

Within this benchmark, \ourmethod{} serves as a hierarchy-aware and novelty-aware reference model rather than a universal winner. Its strongest evidence is a statistically supported gain in coarse-lineage macro-F1 over strong classical baselines and a clear novelty-detection advantage over training-free confidence scores. Its fine-subtype performance remains competitive with, but not significantly superior to, XGBoost. We view this as the right scientific outcome: the benchmark forces a more disciplined account of what is genuinely improved, what remains unresolved, and which claims the current evidence does not support.
