\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding is not that more subjects are unimportant, but that a
standard fixed-total experiment does not estimate their isolated value. Across
three datasets, the label-agnostic design produces an apparently general
subject-breadth advantage. Under fixed class quotas and within-class donor
balancing, mean effects are small and model- and cohort-dependent. This does
not invalidate natural sampling: its gains can include practically valuable
label coverage. It does invalidate treating that gain as an isolated diversity
effect. Patient-disjoint splitting prevents subject overlap; specifying the
allocation policy determines which scientific question the scaling curve answers.

The class-matched protocol deliberately targets a narrower estimand. It holds
observed labels, per-class cell counts, and class proportions fixed while
changing how many subjects contribute those cells. It does not fix
disease--subtype composition, within-class subject heterogeneity, or unrecorded
pool and batch structure. The remaining effect is therefore still
associational, and a near-zero estimate under the tested budgets is not a
universal saturation law. In particular, the existing real-data contrast changes
both label quotas and within-class allocation, so it does not identify their
separate contributions. The synthetic check shows that quota matching need not
mechanically erase a breadth benefit, but does not establish real-data mediation.
The protocol provides a reproducible sensitivity analysis rather than a unique
causal explanation of the observed regularity. The joint stability ranges retain
training-subset variability and cannot establish a zero mean effect or mean
equivalence. Known-target simulations assess target recovery, not the coverage
of these intervals. External RNA experiments additionally use restricted
293-gene representations; broader RNA representations remain a needed sensitivity.

For study design, both targets should be reported: natural allocation estimates
the benefit of the tested recruitment-and-sampling policy, while matched
allocation asks whether that benefit survives a specified label distribution.
Budgets, reference quotas, realized donor coverage, and subset sensitivity
should accompany each curve. Neither a small matched estimate nor a positive
natural estimate alone determines an optimal recruitment policy. Separating
quota matching from donor allocation on real data and constructing calibrated
intervals for seed-averaged effects are the next validation steps.

The 40-patient APT cohort also limits patient-level inference: six disease
groups contain only 6--8 independent patients each. Neither more cells nor
additional resampling supplies independent clinical replication. Nested
validation controls information flow, but does not resolve limited precision,
adaptive analysis choices, or unmeasured acquisition confounding. The disease
audits are consequently exploratory and are not prerequisites for the scaling
contribution. COMBAT and OneK1K extend the scaling evaluation, not the clinical
validation of APT disease prediction. More generally, subject and cell budgets,
label support, split units, and both uncertainty sources should be explicit in
single-cell evaluation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Fixed cell totals can confound subject breadth with the training label
distribution. Across APT, COMBAT, and OneK1K, class matching with within-class
donor balancing changes breadth estimates, while joint resampling exposes their
sensitivity to training subsets and test subjects. APT-Bench makes the
distinction between total and label-standardized allocation effects operational
through a common evaluation contract. This supports more interpretable
subject--cell comparisons without claiming a universal scaling law, equivalence
between sampling strategies, or clinical validity.
