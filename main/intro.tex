\section{Introduction}

Current cancer screening pathways remain fragmented and largely organ-specific, requiring different tests for different cancer types while leaving many malignancies without established population-level screening strategies \citep{duffy2026guide, hoffman2025multicancer, ebbert2025multi}. Suspicious findings often require additional imaging and invasive tissue biopsy for definitive diagnosis. These limitations motivate minimally invasive approaches that can be repeatedly applied across large populations and interrogate multiple cancer types within a single test. Blood-based liquid biopsy provides such an opportunity by capturing multiple cancer-associated signals from a single draw, although its population-level clinical utility remains under active evaluation \citep{bao2025early, schrag2023blood}. Aptamer-based single-cell profiling provides a complementary signal source by quantifying multiplexed cell-surface molecular features at single-cell resolution \citep{wu2025high}. Unlike bulk measurements, single-cell profiling preserves both disease-associated changes in circulating cell populations and the organization of the immune compartment across lineages and subtypes. Recent peripheral blood single-cell studies have identified reproducible cancer-associated changes in immune cell composition and state that support diagnostic prediction \citep{xia2025precise, zhang2024pan}. Consistent with this literature, lineage-resolved analysis of our own APT panel likewise reveals disease-specific aptamer-binding signatures within the T/NK, B, and myeloid compartments (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:apt_markers}), indicating that disease-associated differences are detectable at the molecular level within our cohort and are not confined to shifts in cell-type composition alone. Together, these properties motivate APT profiling as a minimally invasive substrate for integrated multi-cancer detection and immune characterization.

We formulate this setting as a nested prediction problem over disease, lineage, and subtype labels, with each blood-derived cell associated with coordinated predictions at increasing levels of biological granularity. At the disease level, the model performs six way classification among healthy controls (Normal) and five malignancies, including colorectal cancer (CRC), gastric cancer (GC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pancreatic cancer (PC), and biliary tract cancer (BTC). At the lineage level, cells are assigned to T, B, NK, myeloid, or other lineages, while the subtype level distinguishes 27 fine-grained immune cell subtypes. This coarse-to-fine organization reflects the hierarchical structure of cellular identity rather than an arbitrary modeling choice. Recent single-cell annotation methods that explicitly incorporate such taxonomies have reported improved out-of-distribution performance relative to flat classifiers \citep{cultrera2026improving, kumar2025scinfer}, suggesting that hierarchical structure provides useful inductive bias. The three levels also provide complementary clinical information. Disease prediction provides the diagnostic call, while lineage and subtype predictions characterize changes in the circulating immune compartment that have been repeatedly associated with disease across large peripheral blood single-cell studies \citep{nietointerpretable, yang2024pan}. Jointly predicting disease status and immune composition therefore provides a richer readout than a diagnostic label alone.

Accurate classification alone, however, is insufficient for reliable clinical deployment. A population-scale screening system will inevitably encounter cancer types, immune states, or cellular phenotypes that are absent from its training data. Conventional closed-set classifiers must nevertheless assign such inputs to one of the known classes and can produce high-confidence predictions even when the input lies outside the training distribution. In a diagnostic setting, such behavior can lead to unreliable downstream decisions. We therefore treat novelty awareness as a core requirement alongside predictive accuracy. In addition to classification probabilities, the system should estimate whether an input is consistent with the training distribution and signal uncertainty when evidence for a known class is insufficient. This objective follows established work on selective prediction and out-of-distribution detection for trustworthy and medical machine learning \citep{weng2025out, hendrickx2107machine, yang2024generalized}. It is also closely related to the problem of identifying previously unseen cell types in single-cell analysis rather than silently assigning them to known categories \citep{theunissen2024uncertainty, lopez2025conformal}.

We incorporate novelty detection into the representation learned by the hierarchical classifier rather than treating it only as a post-hoc decision rule over the final predicted label. We study two realizations. The first is a training-free approach that derives novelty scores directly from classification logits, while the second uses a dedicated novelty head trained on the shared representation of the cascade. The dedicated head provides substantially stronger novelty discrimination and is therefore adopted in the final model. The novelty head is trained separately from the classification cascade using frozen representations and a nested leave-one-disease-out scheme that explicitly simulates novelty as an entirely unseen disease.

Applying hierarchical and novelty-aware prediction to real clinical data introduces several practical challenges. First, our cohort contains only 40 patients but more than 370,000 single cells, creating a substantial mismatch between patient-level sample size and cell-level observations. Evaluation must therefore prevent cells from the same patient from appearing across training and test sets, since such leakage can substantially overestimate generalization. Second, lineage and subtype labels obey a strict hierarchy, yet independently trained classifiers can produce biologically inconsistent predictions across these levels. Third, the 27 subtype classes are strongly imbalanced, making macro-level performance particularly sensitive to rare classes. Finally, deployment requires the model to recognize inputs from diseases or cellular states absent from training rather than forcing them into the fixed set of known classes. These challenges motivate a framework that combines patient-independent evaluation, hierarchical prediction, and explicit novelty detection.

We address these challenges by introducing, to our knowledge, the first benchmark for hierarchical, novelty-aware classification on aptamer-based single-cell liquid biopsy data, together with a cascade architecture validated on it. Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A patient-independent multi-cancer APT benchmark.} We introduce the first aptamer-based single-cell benchmark for coordinated disease, lineage, and subtype prediction with explicit novelty detection, comprising a real-world cohort of 40 patients and more than 370,000 cells, a patient-level 5-fold cross-validation protocol with pooled bootstrap confidence intervals, a leave-one-disease-out (LODO) generalization suite, and a systematic comparison against five classical machine learning baselines. We additionally quantify how cell-level splitting inflates hierarchical classification performance relative to patient-independent evaluation, underscoring the importance of preventing patient-level leakage in small-cohort single-cell studies.

    \item \textbf{\ourmethod{}, a hierarchical, novelty-aware cascade.} Because disease classification is already near saturation under our patient-independent protocol, \ourmethod{} focuses on the more challenging lineage and subtype levels through differentiable soft routing and a dedicated novelty detection head trained on the shared cascade representation.
\end{itemize}
