\section{Introduction}

Cancer screening and diagnosis are usually framed at the patient level: does a person have disease, and if so which disease category is most likely? Single-cell liquid biopsy changes this framing. A single blood draw can support both a patient-level diagnostic decision and a cell-level characterization of the circulating immune compartment. These outputs are related, but they do not live at the same observational unit. Disease is a property of the patient, whereas lineage and subtype are properties of the cell.

This mismatch matters for evaluation. Our cohort contains more than 370{,}000 single cells, but only 40 patients. Treating cells as independent disease observations would substantially overstate the effective sample size of the diagnostic task. It would also hide a second asymmetry: lineage and subtype form a genuine cell-level hierarchy, but disease does not sit above that hierarchy. The clinically relevant problem is therefore not a single three-level taxonomy. It is a multi-resolution setting in which different targets require different evaluation protocols, inductive biases, and interpretations.

We study this setting on aptamer-based single-cell protein profiling. Aptamer (APT) measurements provide a fixed 293-dimensional panel of protein-target binding signals for each cell, offering a minimally invasive view of both disease-associated molecular state and immune composition from peripheral blood \citep{wu2025high}. This modality is attractive for repeated testing and potentially broad deployment, but it raises three intertwined modeling questions. First, how much predictive information is present at coarse versus fine biological resolution? Second, how sensitive are conclusions to whether evaluation is patient-disjoint or leakage-prone? Third, can a model recognize when a new patient's cells come from a disease category absent from training, rather than forcing them into a closed-set label space?

We address these questions with APT-Bench, a patient-disjoint benchmark for multi-resolution characterization from single-cell APT profiles. The benchmark is organized around five related tasks: patient-level disease recognition, cell-level coarse lineage prediction, cell-level fine subtype prediction, hierarchical path diagnostics, and leave-one-disease-out novelty detection. This framing emphasizes that the central unresolved cell-level problem is not disease classification, which is already nearly saturated within cohort by simple baselines, but reliable hierarchical annotation and novelty awareness under patient-independent evaluation.

To instantiate this benchmark, we introduce \ourmethod{}, a differentiable lineage-subtype cascade with a trained novelty head. The model combines a shared APT encoder, soft lineage routing, subtype experts, and a nested leave-one-disease-out novelty detector. The method is not presented as a universal winner over strong tabular baselines. Instead, it serves as a hierarchy-aware neural reference model that helps reveal where explicit structure matters and where classical methods remain competitive.

This perspective leads to three empirical findings. First, coarse lineage recognition is substantially easier than fine subtype recognition across all models. Second, cell-level splitting inflates both tasks relative to patient-disjoint evaluation, with larger inflation for the neural model than for XGBoost. Third, disease labels are perfectly recoverable under the prespecified within-cohort patient folds, but sensitivity analyses show that this signal depends strongly on patient-specific absolute-expression structure, making overclaiming inappropriate in the absence of acquisition-batch metadata.

Our contributions are therefore as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{APT-Bench, a patient-disjoint multi-resolution benchmark for single-cell APT liquid biopsy.} We formalize disease recognition, lineage prediction, subtype prediction, hierarchy diagnostics, novelty detection, protocol sensitivity, and measurement efficiency within a single evaluation setting over 40 patients and more than 370{,}000 cells.
    \item \textbf{\ourmethod{}, a hierarchy-aware and novelty-aware reference model.} We introduce a soft-routed lineage-subtype cascade with a trained novelty head and show that it improves coarse-lineage macro-F1 over the strongest classical baseline while remaining competitive, but not significantly superior, on fine-subtype macro-F1.
    \item \textbf{A disciplined empirical characterization of what the modality does and does not support.} We show that disease recognition is extremely strong within cohort but sensitive to patient-specific absolute-expression structure, that cell-level evaluation can substantially overstate performance, and that a trained novelty head detects unseen diseases more reliably than training-free confidence scores.
\end{itemize}
