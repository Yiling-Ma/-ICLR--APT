\section{Introduction}

Cancer screening and diagnosis are usually framed at the patient level: does a person have disease, and if so which disease category is most likely? Single-cell liquid biopsy changes this framing. A single blood draw can support both a patient-level diagnostic decision and a cell-level characterization of the circulating immune compartment. These outputs are related, but they do not live at the same observational unit. Disease is a property of the patient, whereas lineage and subtype are properties of the cell.

This mismatch matters for evaluation. Our benchmark contains 361{,}792 single cells, but only 40 patients. Treating cells as independent disease observations would substantially overstate the effective sample size of the diagnostic task. It would also hide a second asymmetry: lineage and subtype form a genuine cell-level hierarchy, but disease does not sit above that hierarchy. The clinically relevant problem is therefore not a single three-level taxonomy. It is a multi-resolution setting in which different targets require different evaluation protocols, inductive biases, and interpretations.

We study aptamer-based single-cell profiling. Apt-seq uses
sequencing-compatible nucleic-acid affinity reagents to quantify molecules such as surface proteins
while retaining cell barcodes and paired transcriptomic state \citep{wu2025high}.
Unlike gene-vocabulary scRNA-seq or antibody-derived-tag integration benchmarks,
our predictive input is a fixed 293-aptamer binding panel. This enables three
questions: how performance changes from coarse lineage to fine subtype; how
patient-level disease evaluation handles many correlated cells but only 40
subjects; and whether cell-level predictions retain patient-associated
variation under strict nested aggregation.

APT-Bench fixes three patient-disjoint tracks: coarse-to-fine cell typing,
two-axis training-scale evaluation, and cross-fitted patient characterization
with explicit technical controls. Aptamer-panel and inference-cell budgets are
reported only as secondary sensitivities of the observed within-cohort signal. Its reusable contribution is not a
claim that one cohort spans deployment, but a common contract for a new modality:
immutable patient folds, explicit observational units, leakage-resistant metrics,
and outer-test-isolated budget controls that can extend to future clinical single-cell cohorts.

The hierarchy track uses a matched reference ladder rather than centering the
paper on a new architecture: XGBoost, a flat Transformer, the same Transformer
with hierarchical cross-entropy (HCE), and a soft-cascade Transformer. The
three neural references share the tokenizer, encoder dimensions, optimization
budget, patient folds, and checkpoint rule. This design asks whether hierarchy
mechanisms add value after controlling encoder capacity; it does not designate
the cascade as a proposed method.

This perspective leads to three empirical findings. First, coarse lineage
recognition is substantially easier than fine subtype recognition across all
models, and encoder-matched controls do not support an advantage for the
soft cascade over the simpler neural references. Second, a two-axis scaling
study identifies label-distribution confounding in fixed-total evaluations. When
each paired setting uses exactly the same observed classes and per-class cell
quotas, LR and XGBoost yield small, model- and cohort-dependent coarse effects
and negative or near-zero fine point estimates; every joint interval over
training subsets and held-out subjects includes zero. By contrast, 37 of 38 unmatched comparisons
favor reallocating cells from 8 to 32 subjects because that intervention also
changes the training label distribution. Subject breadth is therefore a
separate design axis, but an unmatched scaling curve cannot isolate its effect
from task granularity and the marginal class distribution during training.
Third, strictly nested, disease-label-free XGBoost subtype
probabilities retain disease-associated variation after patient aggregation:
soft subtype composition reaches disease macro-F1 0.593 versus 0.477 for
lineage composition. The paired difference is uncertain, however, and subtype
composition does not significantly exceed cell count or a six-feature recorded
RNA-QC baseline. The
bridge is therefore predictive and associational; direct APT discrimination is
stronger but sensitive to patient-specific absolute-expression structure.

Our contributions are therefore as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{APT-Bench, a patient-disjoint benchmark for single-cell APT liquid biopsy.} We formalize fixed patient folds, common inputs and labels, and a portable evaluation contract spanning hierarchy prediction, two-axis scaling, and confounding-aware patient characterization.
    \item \textbf{A controlled account of the granularity gap.} Beyond aggregate macro-F1, we report joint hierarchy metrics, subtype-level support analyses, and patient-clustered comparisons. An encoder-matched reference ladder separates hierarchy mechanisms from backbone capacity and finds no supported gain from additional cascade complexity.
    \item \textbf{A label-distribution audit for two-axis scaling.} Across APT, COMBAT, and OneK1K, we show that standard fixed-total comparisons conflate subject breadth with the marginal training label distribution, then introduce a class-matched control that freezes observed classes and per-class cell quotas. Joint uncertainty over training subsets and held-out subjects leaves no corrected LR or XGBoost effect resolved; external LR frontiers remain unresolved through 64 COMBAT and 128 OneK1K training subjects, ruling out a universal scaling law in these experiments. A separate strict nested audit tests cross-task transfer from disease-label-free cell-model outputs without exposing either stage to outer-test patients.
\end{itemize}
