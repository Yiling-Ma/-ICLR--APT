\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Disease Recognition Is Strong Within Cohort, but Requires Caveats}

We begin with disease because it is the clinically salient target, then immediately separate it from the central modeling contribution. Under the patient-level 5-fold protocol, all five classical cell-level baselines yield the same patient-level majority-vote result: all 40 patients are correctly assigned, for a Clopper-Pearson 95\% interval of $[0.912, 1.000]$ (Table~\ref{tab:disease}). A genuinely patient-level model using each patient's median APT profile also performs strongly, reaching accuracy 0.950 and macro-AUROC 1.000.

These results do not justify a claim of solved clinical diagnosis. Sensitivity analyses in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:disease_patient_level} show that the disease signal depends strongly on patient-specific absolute-expression structure: patient-wise centering reduces patient-level accuracy from 1.000 to 0.650, and a 40-way patient-identity diagnostic reaches 88.4\% accuracy on held-out cells. Because acquisition-batch metadata are unavailable, the observed offsets cannot be cleanly decomposed into biological versus technical sources. We therefore use disease recognition primarily to motivate the need for careful statistical-unit accounting in APT-Bench, not as the main architectural success story.

\subsection{Patient-Disjoint Cell-Level Hierarchy Prediction Remains Difficult}

The harder unresolved problem is cell-level hierarchy prediction under patient-disjoint evaluation. Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that coarse lineage recognition is consistently easier than fine subtype recognition. For \ourmethod{}, coarse macro-F1 reaches 0.333 while fine macro-F1 is only 0.156; for XGBoost, the corresponding values are 0.322 and 0.143. This granularity gap appears across all baselines and indicates that APT captures broad immune identity more reliably than fine subtype structure.

Within this harder setting, \ourmethod{} provides its clearest performance gain at the coarse level. It improves coarse-lineage macro-F1 over XGBoost by 0.0107, and the paired patient-bootstrap confidence interval on the difference excludes zero. At the fine level, \ourmethod{} remains competitive but does not significantly exceed XGBoost. This is the right scope of the claim: explicit hierarchy helps, but it does not create a universal advantage over strong tabular baselines once patient-independent evaluation is enforced.

A coarse--fine consistency diagnostic computed from these same pooled out-of-fold predictions is reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:consistency}. We keep it as supporting evidence because consistency can be altered mechanically by constrained decoding and is not a standalone measure of predictive quality.

\subsection{Tail Performance Is Suggestive, Not Headline Evidence}

Because subtype frequencies are highly imbalanced, we also examine head and tail classes. Table~\ref{tab:tail_head} shows that \ourmethod{} achieves the highest tail macro-F1 point estimate, while XGBoost remains slightly stronger on head classes. However, the tail advantage is not statistically secure under patient-level resampling, so we treat it as a supporting observation rather than a main claim. The important qualitative point is that the relative behavior of methods depends on biological resolution and class regime, not just on a single pooled metric.

\subsection{Protocol Sensitivity: Patient-Level versus Cell-Level Splitting}
\label{sec:results:protocol}

Finally, Table~\ref{tab:protocol_sensitivity} compares the primary patient-disjoint protocol with the cell-level split control. Both \ourmethod{} and XGBoost look stronger under cell-level splitting, but the inflation is larger for \ourmethod{}: +0.099 versus +0.050 on coarse lineage, and +0.105 versus +0.069 on fine subtype. This shows that leakage can change not only absolute performance but also the apparent advantage of a method family. APT-Bench therefore treats patient-disjoint evaluation as a non-negotiable requirement, not a reporting detail.

\subsection{Disease Signal Is Measurement-Efficient but Predictively Distributed}
\label{sec:results:measurement}

The compact-panel analysis in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:minimal_panel} provides a final perspective on what the APT modality supports. Under a nested patient-level selection pipeline, all five outer folds choose the same development tolerance budget, $B^*=10$, and the resulting 10-aptamer panels reproduce the full-panel patient-level macro-F1 of 1.0 for both Logistic Regression and XGBoost on every outer test fold. A complementary equal-cell-per-patient subsampling diagnostic, computed from held-out disease predictions without retraining, preserves 100\% patient-level accuracy across 50 random trials with only 10 cells per patient. At the same time, random panels approach full performance as the aptamer budget increases, and removing many top-ranked aptamers often leaves disease performance nearly unchanged. The correct interpretation is therefore not that the dataset isolates a unique minimal biomarker set, but that within-cohort disease information is highly redundant and can be recovered with substantially reduced aptamer and cell-count budgets.

This result complements, rather than weakens, the main disease caveat. The fact that disease labels are recoverable from a compact panel does not resolve whether the underlying signal is biological, technical, or mixed. What it does show is that the within-cohort disease task is easier than the patient-disjoint cell-level hierarchy task, both statistically and operationally. That asymmetry is one of the central empirical messages of APT-Bench.
